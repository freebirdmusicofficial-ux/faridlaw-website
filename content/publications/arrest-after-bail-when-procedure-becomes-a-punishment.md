Yahya Farid Khwaja

The writer is a Lawyer based in Islamabad

 

The rearrest of Imaan Mazari and Hadi AliChattha, only hours after the Supreme Court ordered their release, is difficultto view as anything other than a travesty of justice. At the very least, thesequence creates the unmistakable appearance that criminal procedure is beingused by State not merely to investigate an offence, but as an instrument toensure their continued incarceration. 



Courts may grant bail, sentences may besuspended and release orders may be issued, but little remains of judicialprotection if an eighteen month old case can immediately be activated to returnthe same individuals to prison. The law gives the State coercive powers forinvestigation and prosecution but it does not give it a weapon of choice todefeat liberty of a human being. 



One may disagree with Imaan Mazari’s politics,rhetoric or style of advocacy. For many, her choice of words has often beenconfrontational and capable of aggravating ethnic divisions or underminingpublic institutions. However, disapproval of an individual’s politics cannotbecome a substitute for due process of law. Whatever one thinks of Imaan’sadvocacy, the manner in which her liberty and that of Hadi Chattha have beenrepeatedly curtailed demands legal scrutiny.



On January 24, 2026, an Islamabad sessionscourt convicted the couple under Sections 9, 10 and 26-A PECA in a case arisingfrom social media posts and sentenced them to terms totalling seventeen years.They challenged those convictions before the Islamabad High Court. Afterprolonged proceedings, the matter reached the Supreme Court. On September 17,after more than  a two-member benchcomprising Justice Naeem Akhtar Afghan and Justice Ishtiaq Ibrahim suspendedthe operation of the trial court judgment and ordered both lawyers released onbail until their appeals before the IHC are decided.



Their liberty lasted only hours. IslamabadPolice rearrested the couple in connection with an FIR registered at KohsarPolice Station on March 22, 2025 around eighteen months earlier. The FIRaccused them of anti-government sloganeering, blocking roads and obstructingofficials, invoking Sections 188, 341, 506, 353 and 186 of the Pakistan PenalCode. Crucially, no provision of the Anti-Terrorism Act was included when theFIR was originally registered. Yet following their re-arrest, Mazari andChattha were produced before an Anti-Terrorism Court late that night.



The prosecution then sought an extraordinary30 days of physical remand which was refused by ATC Judge Abul Hasnat MuhammadZulqarnain. The court noted that while the FIR alleged membership of anunlawful assembly, no weapon had been attributed to either accused and thedemand for physical remand did not appear plausible. Instead, the court sentthem to judicial custody for 14 days. Thus, within hours of the Supreme Courtsecuring their release from incarceration, they found themselves back in jail, thistime under an eighteen month old FIR for which the police had failed topersuade even the remand court that custodial interrogation was necessary.



This chronology raises the first fundamentallegal problem. An FIR does not create an automatic obligation on the State andLaw Enforcement to arrest. In *Mst Sughran Bibi v The State* (PLD 2018 SC595), the Supreme Court held that ordinarily a person should not be arrestedmerely because he or she has been nominated in an FIR. The investigatingofficer must be satisfied that sufficient justification for arrest exists. Thatprinciple becomes especially important here. If custodial arrest was genuinelynecessary, why did it take eighteen months for the State to realize that? 



Mazari and Chattha were hardly absconders.They were known to the authorities, litigating before courts and, for months,incarcerated. The unexplained timing points out the kind of surroundingcircumstance from which mala fide may legitimately be examined. The SupremeCourt has itself recognised in *Khalil Ahmed Soomro v The State* (PLD 2017SC 730) that mala fide is often incapable of direct proof and may be inferredfrom facts and circumstances. That the arrest was made on the evening theSupreme Court granted bail in the PECA case is the kind of circumstances thatclearly points out towards malafide in this whole affair. 



There is an equally troubling patternmwitnessed in the last couple of years. Criminal procedure cannot be employedfor a collateral purpose. If one dormant proceeding is activated immediatelyafter another court orders release, the question is whether the second arrestserves a genuine investigative need or merely neutralises the first court’sorder. The latter would reduce liberty to a procedural illusion. The State mayinvestigate multiple cases, but it cannot legitimately operate a revolving doorof detention in which every judicial release is answered by another arrest.That would transform criminal procedure from a mechanism of justice into thepunishment itself.



Finally, the involvement of the Anti-TerrorismCourt requires explanation. If the FIR originally contained no ATA provision,when was terrorism added, on what material, and how are the statutoryingredients satisfied? In *Ghulam Hussain v The State* (PLD 2020 SC 61),the Supreme Court made clear that even grave criminal conduct does notautomatically constitute terrorism; the action must satisfy the specific designand purpose required by Section 6 of the ATA. Here, the remand judge himselfnoted that no weapon was attributed to the accused and just being part of anunlawful assembly, does not itself constitue an offence of terrorism. 



Lastly, if the couple could appear before theSupreme Court, the High Court and other courts while litigating their cases, aserious question arises as to why judicial custody was indispensable at all.Justice cannot be reduced to keeping an accused behind bars first and findingthe procedural vehicle later.
