Yahya Farid Khwaja

The writer is a Lawyer based in Islamabad

 

Plans for talksbetween PTI and the federal government appear to have eased, for now, theimmediate risk of an emergency or governor’s rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa.Neither option, however, has been ruled out. Such interventions haveprecedents, and courts have upheld them subject to constitutional limits, as inthe 1994 Pir Sabir Shah case concerning the then NWFP. The renewed debatetherefore warrants scrutiny: what developments have prompted the Centre toconsider these exceptional measures, and do they establish a constitutionalbasis for intervention in an elected provincial government?

Article 232 permitsthe President to proclaim an emergency where war, external aggression orinternal disturbance beyond provincial control creates a grave threat to thesecurity of Pakistan or any part of it. For internal disturbance, theConstitution requires a Provincial Assembly resolution, while providing that aproclamation issued without it must receive approval from each ParliamentaryHouse (National Assembly and Senate) within ten days. An emergency underArticle 232 expands Federal legislative and executive authority, includingpowers to direct or assume provincial executive functions, but does notautomatically remove the provincial government.

Article 234,commonly associated with Governor’s rule, applies where provincial governmentcannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution. On receiving a Governor’sreport and being satisfied that this condition exists, the President may assumeprovincial executive functions or direct the governor to exercise them on hisbehalf, and place provincial legislative powers under Parliament’s authority.The proclamation expires after two months unless approved by a joint sitting,with extensions subject to an overall six-month limit. Its defining conditionis failure of constitutional machinery, which must be established beyond merelyalleging political disagreement or poor governance.

The most directlyrelevant precedent for KPK is the proclamation of February 25, 1994, placingthe then North West Frontier Province under governor’s rule. In *Pir SabirShah v Federation of Pakistan* (PLD 1994 SC 738), the Supreme Court upheldthe temporary assumption of provincial functions but, by a seven-to-twomajority, held that the President could not remove the chief minister andcabinet beyond the proclamation’s duration. The judgment recognised temporaryintervention while rejecting permanent dismissal through that instrument. Otherprovinces have experienced similar interventions in different circumstances.Governor’s rule was imposed in Sindh in 1998 amid political conflict andviolence in Karachi. In Punjab, it followed the Sharif brothers’disqualification in February 2009 and was lifted the following month.

Balochistan providesa more recent security-related example. On January 14, 2013, governor’s rulewas imposed following the Quetta bombings and protests by the Hazara community,displacing Nawab Aslam Raisani’s administration and placing executive authoritywith Governor Nawab Zulfikar Ali Magsi. Raisani’s government stood restoredwhen the two-month period expired in March. The episode illustrates thetemporary character of intervention under Article 234, although itscircumstances cannot automatically justify a similar measure in KP.

The Centre has citeddeteriorating security and alleged governance failures in KP as reasons forconsidering governor’s rule or an emergency. Federal ministers accuse theprovincial administration of inadequately supporting counterterrorism effortsand failing to provide sufficient personnel, equipment and resources to theCounter-Terrorism Department. Their argument is that the provincial leadershiphas neglected its responsibility to protect citizens and support lawenforcement. These allegations, however, would need to be supported by evidenceconnecting particular administrative failures to the constitutional grounds forintervention.

The planned march onIslamabad has added a political dimension to the confrontation. Rana Sanaullahhas portrayed it as a provincial government acting against the capital,questioning whether an elected administration can use its position to supportsuch mobilisation. KP disputes the Centre’s allegations, attributes continuinginsecurity to a flawed counterterrorism policy and maintains that its protestwould be peaceful. The legal assessment must therefore distinguish the exerciseof political rights from any demonstrable unlawful use of provincial authorityor public resources. The announcement of a march alone cannot settle thatquestion.

These competingclaims must be assessed against the distinct constitutional thresholds. For anemergency based on internal disturbance, Article 232 requires a grave threat tosecurity beyond the provincial government’s capacity to control. Article 234 requirescircumstances in which provincial government cannot continue in accordance withthe Constitution. Allegations of poor governance, policy disagreement orpolitical protest do not, by themselves, establish either condition. The Centrewould need to explain how the evidence satisfies the provision it invokes,while KP could counter with a record showing that its institutions remainfunctional and capable of discharging their constitutional responsibilities.

Any legal challengewould first face Article 236(2), which expressly bars courts from questioningproclamations and orders under the Constitution’s emergency provisions.Nevertheless, *Pir Sabir Shah* recognises scope for scrutinising actiontaken without jurisdiction, beyond constitutional authority or in bad faith. KPcould argue that the stated reasons do not meet the relevant threshold, thatmandatory procedures were disregarded, or that the power was exercised for anulterior political purpose. Such arguments would require specific particularsand supporting evidence. Their success would depend on establishing a legallyreviewable defect, rather than merely disputing the Centre’s political assessment.

The establishment ofthe Federal Constitutional Court (FCC) has changed the forum for challenginggovernor’s rule, while leaving Article 236(2)’s restriction on judicial reviewintact. A dispute between the KP government and the Federation would fall withinthe FCC’s exclusive jurisdiction under Article 175E(1), with relief limited toa declaratory judgment under Article 175E(2). However, amended Article 189means that earlier Supreme Court decisions, including *Pir Sabir Shah*and *Farooq Leghari*, do not bind the FCC. KP could rely on theirrecognition of review for lack or excess of jurisdiction and bad faith, but theFCC would ultimately determine the scope of those exceptions under the presentconstitutional framework.
